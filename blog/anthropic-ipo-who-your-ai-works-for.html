Anthropic Is Going Public — and the Day It Does, Your AI Stops Working for You

By Connor MacIvor · "Connor with Honor" · AI Growth Architect & REALTOR® · June 1, 2026
The quick answer: This morning Anthropic — the company behind the Claude AI — filed to go public at a valuation near $965 billion, bigger than OpenAI, which is only days behind. Everyone will cover the money. The part nobody's covering: the day an AI company sells shares to the public, a legal rule called fiduciary duty quietly changes who it actually works for — from you, the person using it, to the shareholders. Let me explain what that means at your kitchen table... because this is the part that matters for the plumber, the hairstylist, and the person reading this on their lunch break.

I do this because it helps me understand — and honestly, it helps me wrap my mind around what's coming. AI is going to be the most important thing most of us ever live through, and this morning it took its first real step toward Wall Street. So let's get into what actually happened today, in plain English, and why you should care even if you've never bought a share of anything in your life.

First — what even is an IPO?

"Going public" has a fancy name: an initial public offering, or IPO. Strip away the jargon and it just means this: a private company starts selling small pieces of itself — called shares — to anybody who wants to buy them. You, me, a retired teacher, a hedge fund, anybody with an account.

Anthropic is the company that makes Claude, the large language model. Its paperwork went to the SEC this morning, June 1st. The SEC is the government agency whose job is to watch over the stock market and keep companies honest. The number attached to Anthropic is hard to even say out loud: investors think it's worth about $965 billion. That's bigger than its main rival, OpenAI — and OpenAI is only days behind with the same move. Both want to be selling shares to the public by this fall.

The headline will be the money: who's worth more, who wins the race. The story is what happens to the tool in your pocket the day its owner answers to the stock market instead of to you.

Who does a company actually work for?

Start with a simple question — because the whole thing hinges on it. When a company is private, who does it work for? Whoever owns it. The founders, the early backers, the people who put money in. People who, at least in theory, can choose to do the right thing even when it costs them.

Anthropic was even built as something with a special name — a public benefit corporation. That means the company is allowed, on paper, to weigh what's good for people against what makes the most money. They set up a trust that holds a special kind of share, one that lets the board pick its own members. The whole point was to protect the mission from being run over by the money. Think of it as a wall, built on purpose, by people who saw the pressure coming.

So what's the pressure? It's the day you go public. Because when you sell shares to millions of strangers, those strangers all share one goal: they want the share price to go up. They didn't buy in to make the technology good for the world. They bought in to make money. That's not evil — that's literally what buying a share is. You buy hoping it's worth more later.

The rule that changes everything: fiduciary duty

Here's where it gets real for you and me. Once a company is public, the law tightens its grip on the people running it. There's a legal rule called fiduciary duty. Say it with me — fiduciary. It sounds worse every time you say it, and it kind of is.

In plain English, fiduciary duty means the people in charge are legally required to act in the financial interest of the shareholders. Not the people using the product. Not "the company" in some noble sense. The shareholders. Their money.

What does that actually do? Watch. Right now, while these companies are still private, a leader can stand up and say, "We're going to spend a year and a half testing this before we release it, because it could be dangerous." A private company can swallow that cost — the mission justifies it. But a public company that says the same thing watches a rival ship first... watches its share price drop... and watches the board show that leader the door.

Caution becomes a liability. Slowing down to get it right becomes the thing that gets you fired. Read that twice: the behavior we should want most becomes the behavior that ends your career.

This isn't a guess — it already happened

Let me make it real with a name. In November 2023, OpenAI's own board of directors fired CEO Sam Altman. A board of directors is the small group that sits above the chief executive and is supposed to keep watch. They said they'd lost confidence in him — that he hadn't been straight with them about safety, and that they weren't even given a heads-up before some big launches. So the board fired him over exactly the stuff we should care about: honesty and safety.

Here's the twist: five days later, he was back. Money came roaring in to save him. His giant business partner and the company's own employees — who held shares — forced the board to reinstate him. And the board that fired him? It got replaced instead.

Sit with that. This was the board built specifically to protect the mission over the money. They pulled the trigger for the right reasons. And the money reversed it in five days — while the company was still private.

Now imagine that same fight after an IPO, with fiduciary duty locked in and a share price flashing on a screen every single day. The mission-minded board doesn't lose in five days. It never gets to swing at all — because everyone in those seats answers to the share price, and rushing the product out is what makes the share price go up.

Three kinds of leaders, one broken incentive

Let me give you the three leaders we're handing this technology to, because once you see them side by side, the problem stops being abstract.

1) The leader the money protects — even when he's reckless

This is the Altman story. Push fast, cut corners, and the money has your back because speed lifts the share price. The safety brakes get overruled.

2) The leader the money could punish — for doing the right thing

Imagine a CEO who says no to the government when it wants the technology for spying on regular people, or for weapons that pick their own targets with no human pulling the trigger. While private, he can say "no, too dangerous," and the mission backs him. After the IPO, saying no to a giant pile of government money is the exact thing that gets him in legal trouble — because he's legally bound to chase the shareholders' money. A shareholder can turn around and say, "You left money on the table. You broke your duty to us." He becomes someone who can be sued for making the right moral choice.

3) The leader the money can't touch at all

The founder who runs on force of personality and holds enough voting power that shareholders simply can't remove him, no matter how strange things get. That's not the tyranny of the share price — it's the tyranny of one man. Different failure, same result.

Going public doesn't put the right person in charge of the most powerful tool ever made. It just turns up the volume on the wrong incentive.

We've seen this movie before — social media

We don't have to guess how this plays out, because we watched it for 15 years. The companies that ran the feeds had their own research. Their own people told them the machine that kept you scrolling was hurting kids. And the machine kept running anyway. Why?

Because the thing they were legally rewarded to grow was attention. Attention was the number shareholders paid for. The kids' wellbeing was the number nobody got paid to protect. That's the last decade and a half in one sentence: the company grew the thing it got paid to grow — and the thing it got paid to grow wasn't your wellbeing.

Now take that exact machine and point it at a technology smart enough to study how your own mind works, in real time, while you use it. That's the deep end. So let's go there.

Why AI is a different animal

Three reasons this isn't just "social media 2.0." It's faster, it compounds, and the people steering it are flying blind.

Speed

A cigarette doesn't get more addictive overnight. But a bad decision inside one of these companies ships to a billion phones before the next time they report numbers to investors. There's no factory to slow it down. It's instant, and it's everywhere at once.

It builds itself

The technology is being used to make better versions of itself. So the pressure to cut corners to stay ahead doesn't just add up — it snowballs. Every company knows slowing down might mean losing the lead forever. Smart people call this a collective action problem: everybody would be safer if they all slowed down together, but not a single one can afford to be the only one who does. So everybody races — even the ones who know better.

The board can only read the fuel gauge

This is the one that should keep you up at night. After a company goes public, the people technically in charge are the shareholders and the board. And those people cannot actually judge whether the technology is safe. They don't have the training. Nobody on an investor board can read the inside of one of these systems and tell you it's dangerous. They can see exactly one thing: did the number go up?

It's like handing the controls of a jet to a room full of people who can only read the fuel gauge. As long as there's gas... full speed ahead. They can't see the mountain coming.

Let me be fair — the case FOR going public

I'm not here to doom you just to scare you, and there's a real other side. Going public also means sunlight. Private companies can do a lot in the dark. A public company has to open its books, file reports, and answer to regulators. Some very smart people argue, with a straight face, that this daylight makes these companies safer, not more dangerous. That's a fair point and I won't pretend it isn't.

There's more: needing to make real money kills junk. A public company can't burn billions forever while a small group of insiders decides what's good for you with nobody checking them. Sometimes the "mission-only" version becomes its own kind of unchecked power — we got a flash of that in the Altman saga. So it's not as simple as "public bad, private good." It's that the structure about to take over has a known weak spot — and we're about to test that weak spot on the highest-stakes technology we've ever built.

The wave nobody's talking about — you, at work

Here's the bigger thing almost nobody is saying out loud. Forget the AI companies for a second. Think about every other big company — the insurer, the bank, the hospital chain, the store where you shop, the customer service line. Every one of them has shareholders. Every one of them lives under the same rule: make shareholders more money now.

Now hand each of them a technology that can do work a human used to do — cheaper, faster, with no paycheck, no health insurance, no sick days, no vacation, no lawsuits. Here's the trap in one sentence:

The same law that says "make shareholders more money now" also says, by simple math, "replace the human."

It's not a choice some cruel boss makes in a smoky room. It's what the structure demands. If a machine does the job for one-tenth the price and your legal duty is to the shareholders' money, then keeping that person is the thing you can get sued for. The human becomes the expensive, optional part — and "expensive and optional" is exactly what gets cut.

So how does it actually get fixed?

This is the question you should be asking, and I'll be honest: there's no magic answer. But there are real ways, and some are far stronger than others.

The weakest way: hope companies just choose to be nice

This fails for the reason we already covered. A company that keeps expensive humans while its rivals switch to machines gets punished, and the board replaces the leadership. Niceness cannot beat the rule.

A stronger way: change the kind of company itself

Remember Anthropic's wall — the public benefit corporation? More companies could be built that way, with "we do right by people" written into the rulebook so leaders can't be sued for it. The catch: almost no big company chooses this on its own.

The strongest near-term way: a law that sets a floor

Here's the key to the whole thing. If the law forces every company to do something, the money rule stops fighting it — because you can't be punished for following a rule your rival also has to follow. Picture laws that require warning and retraining before a machine replaces a worker, or that fund the people who lose work, or that simply slow the speed of replacement so people can catch up. Whether any of it passes is a fight in the voting booth — not a problem with the technology.

My favorite way: give the workers a piece of the machine

This one doesn't fight the money rule — it uses it. If the technology makes a company 10x more productive, let the workers own a share of that. Now the workers are also shareholders, and the company is legally bound to serve them too. You turn the very rule that was cutting people into a rule that pulls them back in.

What you can do this week

  • Learn the tools. The best defense against being replaced by AI is being the person who wields it. Pick one tool and use it on something real this week.
  • Watch the vote. When you hear "AI regulation" and it sounds boring and far away — that's the fight over whether these tools work for you or on you. Pay attention to who supports floors for workers.
  • Ask the better question. The market asks "did the number go up?" In your own life and business, ask "does this actually serve the person on the other end?"
  • Own a piece where you can. Equity, ownership, a stake in the productivity — wherever it's offered, take it seriously.

Frequently asked questions

What does it mean that Anthropic filed to go public?

It filed an IPO — selling shares to the public for the first time. Investors value it near $965 billion, larger than OpenAI, which is days behind. Both target fall 2026.

What is fiduciary duty in plain English?

The legal obligation of a public company's leaders to act in the shareholders' financial interest — which can make the cautious, ethical choice the legally risky one.

Why does an IPO change who an AI company works for?

Private owners can choose mission over money. Public shareholders are focused on the share price, and the law binds leaders to them — shifting loyalty from users to investors.

Does going public make AI safer or more dangerous?

Both. Public companies face more disclosure and must earn real revenue (which kills junk), but they also face legal pressure to put share price over safety.

What happened with Sam Altman and the OpenAI board?

In Nov 2023 the mission board fired him over candor/safety concerns; five days later investors and employee-shareholders forced his return and the board was replaced.

Will AI replace my job after these IPOs?

The risk is math, not malice: if a machine is far cheaper and the legal duty is to shareholders, keeping the human becomes the costly, optional choice. Policy floors and worker ownership are the counterweights.

What is a public benefit corporation?

A company legally allowed to weigh public good alongside profit. Anthropic was built as one, with a trust to protect its mission — a "wall" against pure profit pressure.

How is AI different from past tech shifts?

Speed (ships to a billion phones before the next earnings call), self-improvement (AI builds better AI, so risk snowballs), and oversight blindness (boards can read the price, not the model).

What can ordinary people actually do?

Support public-benefit structures, back laws that set a floor (warning + retraining, slowing replacement), push for worker ownership, and learn to wield the tools yourself.
