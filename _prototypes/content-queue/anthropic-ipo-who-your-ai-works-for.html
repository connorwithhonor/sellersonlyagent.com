Anthropic Is Going Public — and the Day It Does, Your AI Stops Working for You

By Connor MacIvor · "Connor with Honor" · AI Growth Architect & REALTOR®
Quick answer: This morning Anthropic — the maker of Claude — filed for an IPO at a roughly $965 billion valuation, bigger than OpenAI, which is only days behind. The headline is the money. The part nobody's telling you: the day an AI company sells shares to the public, a legal rule called fiduciary duty changes who it actually works for — from you, the user, to the shareholders. Here's what that means at your kitchen table.

The most powerful technology ever built just took its first step toward Wall Street. Anthropic sent its paperwork to the SEC on June 1st. OpenAI is right behind it. Both want to be selling shares to the public by this fall.

Everyone's going to argue about who's worth more and who wins the race. That's the headline. I want to talk about the thing underneath it — what happens to the tool in your pocket the day its owner has to answer to the stock market instead of to you.

Who does a company actually work for?

When a company is private, it works for whoever owns it — founders, early backers, people who can, at least in theory, choose to do the right thing even when it costs money. Anthropic was even built as a public benefit corporation, with a trust structure designed to protect the mission from being run over by the money. They saw the pressure coming and tried to build a wall against it.

What's the pressure? It's the day you go public. Because then you've sold pieces of the company to millions of strangers, and those strangers share one goal: they want the share price to go up. That's not evil — that's just what owning a share is.

The rule that changes everything: fiduciary duty

Once a company is public, the law tightens its grip. There's a legal obligation called fiduciary duty — the people in charge are legally required to act in the shareholders' financial interest. Not the people using the product. The shareholders.

The behavior we should want most — slowing down to get it right — becomes the behavior that can get you fired.

A private company can stand up and say, "we're going to spend 18 months testing this before we release it, because it could be dangerous," and swallow the cost. A public company that says the same thing watches a rival ship first, watches its share price drop, and watches the board show the leader the door.

This already happened — the five-day story

In November 2023, OpenAI's own board fired Sam Altman, saying he hadn't been straight with them about safety. The board built specifically to protect the mission pulled the trigger for exactly the right reasons. Five days later he was back — money and employee-shareholders forced it, and the board that fired him got replaced instead.

Sit with that. The mission-minded board lost in five days while the company was still private. Now imagine that same fight after an IPO, with fiduciary duty locked in and a share price on the screen every single day. The mission board doesn't lose in five days — it never gets to swing at all.

Three leaders, one broken incentive

Look at the landscape: one leader the money protects even when he's reckless; one leader the money could punish for doing the right thing (say no to dangerous government money and a shareholder can claim you breached your duty); and one leader the money can't control at all because he holds the votes. Three different stories, one root cause: going public doesn't put the right person in charge — it just turns up the volume on the wrong incentive.

We've seen this movie — social media

The companies that ran the feeds had their own research telling them the machine was hurting kids. The machine kept running. Why? Because the thing they were legally rewarded to grow was attention. Attention was what shareholders paid for. Now port that exact machine onto a technology smart enough to study how your mind works in real time, while you use it.

Why AI is different — and more dangerous

Speed: a bad decision inside one of these companies ships to a billion phones before the next earnings call. It builds itself: AI is used to make better AI, so corner-cutting snowballs — everyone races because slowing down might mean losing the lead forever (smart people call it a "collective action problem"). And the board can't read it: the people legally steering can only see one gauge — did the number go up?

It's like handing the controls of a jet to a room that can only read the fuel gauge. As long as there's gas… full speed ahead. They can't see the mountain.

The fair counterpoint

I won't only doom. Going public also means sunlight — public companies must open their books, file reports, and answer to regulators. And needing real revenue kills junk; a mission-only company can become its own kind of unchecked power (we got a flash of that in the Altman saga). So it isn't simply "public bad, private good." It's that the structure about to take over has a known weak spot — and we're about to test it on the highest-stakes technology we've ever built.

The bigger wave nobody says out loud

Forget the AI companies for a second. Every bank, insurer, hospital chain, and store has shareholders and lives under the same rule: make shareholders more money now. Hand each of them a technology that does a human's job cheaper and faster — no paycheck, no benefits, no sick days — and the same law that says "make shareholders more money" also says, by simple math, "replace the human."

It's not a cruel boss in a smoky room. It's the structure. If your legal duty is to the shareholders' money, keeping the expensive human is the thing you can get sued for.

So how does it actually get fixed?

Not by hoping companies choose to be nice — niceness can't beat the rule. The real levers:

  • Change the company itself — more public benefit corporations, where "do right by people" is written into the rulebook so leaders can't be sued for it.
  • Laws that set a floor — if every company must give workers warning and retraining before a machine replaces them, the money rule stops fighting it, because your rival has to follow the same rule.
  • Give workers a piece of the machine (my favorite, because it doesn't fight the money rule — it uses it). If the technology makes the company 10x more productive, let workers own a share. Now the company is legally bound to serve them too. The very rule that was cutting people turns into one that pulls them back in.

Why I care — the kitchen table

I use these tools every single day — in real estate, in voice technology, in software for small businesses. I'm not watching from the cheap seats; I've got my hands in the machine, and the machine is good — the best leverage a one-person operation has ever had. But I never forget who built it or who they're about to answer to. The stock market asks exactly one question: did the number go up? So we ask the one they won't on the investor call — does this actually work for the person reading this on their lunch break?

FAQ

What is Anthropic's IPO valuation?

Investors value Anthropic around $965 billion — larger than rival OpenAI, which is expected to file within days. Both target going public by fall 2026.

What is fiduciary duty in plain English?

It's the legal obligation of a public company's leaders to act in the shareholders' financial interest — which can make the cautious, ethical choice the legally risky one.

Does going public make AI safer or more dangerous?

Both arguments are real. Public companies face more disclosure and "sunlight," but they also face legal pressure to prioritize share price over safety. The structure has a known weak spot we're about to stress-test.

What can ordinary people do?

The rules were written by people and can be rewritten — through public-benefit structures, laws that set a floor for workers, and worker ownership. It's a fight at the kitchen table and the voting booth, not a law of nature.
