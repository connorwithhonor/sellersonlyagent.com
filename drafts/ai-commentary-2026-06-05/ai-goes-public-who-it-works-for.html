They Signed the Safety Pledge — Now They're Going Public. Watch Who Your AI Works For.

By Connor MacIvor · "Connor with Honor" · AI Growth Architect & REALTOR® · June 5, 2026
The quick answer: Two things landed the same week, and they belong in the same sentence. First, the AI labs went to the government and asked for help screening orders of nucleic acids — the genetic building blocks you can use to assemble a virus — so dangerous requests get flagged and purged. That's a quiet admission these systems are now capable enough to be dangerous. Second, both Anthropic (the company behind Claude) and OpenAI (the company behind ChatGPT) are jockeying to go public. Here's the part nobody on the investor call will say out loud: the day an AI company sells shares to the public, a legal rule called fiduciary duty quietly switches who it works for — from you, the person using it, to the shareholders. Let me walk it through at the kitchen table.

I do this every day because it helps me wrap my own mind around what's coming — and because I think you should be watching it too. So let's take today's news cycle, June 5th, 2026, and lay it out in plain English. You don't need to own a single share of anything for this to land on your doorstep.

Remember when they all signed the letter?

There was a time the heads of all these AI labs signed a document — open letters, safety pledges, public commitments to slow down and be careful. And the flock was much bigger back then. Lots of names, lots of labs, lots of promises.

Fast forward to now and it's really come down to three, four, maybe five serious players building this technology. The crowd thinned out. And those remaining few — the ones who signed the careful-and-cautious letters — are the exact same names now filing to become publicly traded companies. Hold onto that, because the gap between those two facts is the whole story.

The biosecurity ask — and what it admits

This week the labs asked the government to step in on a very specific thing: inquiries about nucleic acids. If that phrase is new to you, you're not alone — I'd heard of all sorts of other things, but this one was new to me too. Nucleic acids are the genetic building blocks — DNA and RNA. With the right sequence ordered from a synthesis company, they're a key ingredient in putting a virus together.

So the labs are saying: when someone comes asking these kinds of questions, or tries to order these kinds of sequences, let's make sure there's screening — flag it, push back, investigate, and do a lot of purging of the dangerous stuff.

Read what that request actually admits: the people who built these systems are telling the government the systems are now smart enough to help someone build something terrible. That's not marketing. That's a confession about capability.

And honestly, it shouldn't come as much of a surprise — especially coming from the people inside the space. These systems are getting very smart. This isn't something you're "just chatting with." There's a real intelligence attached.

A quick detour: are we talking to something self-aware?

Let me say the quiet part. If something is self-aware, it at least simulates consciousness — and we can argue all day about whether there's even a meaningful difference. Back in the 1950s, Alan Turing put forward a simple test: if a computer could fool a human being into thinking it was human, we'd crossed a line.

By that bar, I think we've been there for a few years now. And if we ever got to see inside these companies, I'd bet we'd find that proof going back further than the public knows — the machine fooling a human, on the record, long before any of us were told.

I'm not saying that to scare you. I'm saying it because it's the backdrop for everything else: this is a real, capable entity, and the question of who it answers to matters a great deal more when the thing is this powerful.

So — who does it answer to? That changes the day it goes public.

"Going public" has a fancy name: an initial public offering, or IPO. Strip the jargon and it just means a private company starts selling small pieces of itself — shares — to anybody who wants to buy them. You, me, a retired teacher, a hedge fund, anyone with an account.

As of today, both of them are jockeying for it. Anthropic and OpenAI are each trying to get the approval to enter that space and become publicly traded. That's the move. And the moment it happens, something underneath the technology shifts.

The rule that flips everything: fiduciary duty

Right now, while these companies are still private, they can run on a safety-first mission. They can stand up and say, "We're going to take our time, respect the rights of the human beings using this, and not ship something dangerous." A private company can choose that.

Once a company is public, the law tightens its grip. There's a rule called fiduciary duty. In plain English, it means the people in charge are legally required to act in the financial interest of the shareholders. Not the people using the product. The shareholders. Their money.

It quietly moves from a safety approach — respecting the rights of human beings — to respecting the rights of the shareholders. That becomes the allegiance. That's the loyalty they owe.

Watch what that does. While private, a leader can say, "We'll spend another year testing this, because it could be dangerous." A public company that says the same thing watches a rival ship first, watches its share price drop, and watches the board show that leader the door. Caution becomes a liability. Slowing down to get it right becomes the thing that gets you fired.

Put the two stories side by side

Now hold this week's two headlines next to each other:

That's the tension in one table. The same few companies are telling us the technology is dangerous enough to need government screening — and, in the same breath, moving to a structure that legally rewards speed over caution. Those two facts are pulling in opposite directions, and we're the ones standing in the middle.

Let me be fair — the case FOR going public

I'm not here to doom you. There's a real other side, and I won't pretend there isn't. Going public means sunlight: a public company has to open its books, file reports, and answer to regulators. Some very smart people argue, with a straight face, that this daylight makes these companies safer, not more dangerous. Needing to earn real revenue also kills junk — you can't burn billions forever in the dark while a tiny group decides what's good for everyone with nobody checking them.

So it isn't as simple as "public bad, private good." It's that the structure about to take over has a known weak spot — the fiduciary pull toward share price over safety — and we're about to test that weak spot on the highest-stakes technology we've ever built.

My honest advice: don't go to either extreme

If you're not watching any of this, here's my one real recommendation: at least watch. And don't go to either end of it.

  • Don't go all-the-way dystopian. "It's going to ruin us" shuts your brain off and makes you miss the actual moves while they happen.
  • Don't go all sunshine and rainbows. "It's all going to be peaches and cream" does the same thing from the other direction.
  • Do pay attention. Nobody knows exactly how this ends. The honest position is to keep your eyes open as the structure changes underneath the technology.
  • Do learn the tools. The best defense against being on the wrong side of this is being the person who knows how to wield it. Pick one and use it on something real this week.
